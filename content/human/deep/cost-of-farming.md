## What happened

Farming produced more food from each patch of land, and the number of people grew. But for the people who lived through the change, it was not simply an improvement. Early farmers in many regions were shorter than the foragers before them, worked harder, and were sicker. Their villages were crowded, close to their animals and to their own waste. Over the following millennia, farming societies also became more unequal.

## How we know

The evidence is mostly in bones and teeth. A review of stature studies published since 1984 found that, in most cases, adult height fell as people came to rely on farming. This was seen in Europe, Africa, the Middle East, Asia, South America and North America, whenever farming arrived. An earlier survey had found declining health in 19 of 21 societies that made the change.

Çatalhöyük in Turkey gives a close-up view. In a sample of 93 skulls, 25 people had healed fractures, and 12 of them had been hurt more than once; more than half of the victims were women. About 10 to 13 percent of adult teeth had cavities, from a diet heavy in grain. Up to a third of people from the early period show signs of infection on their bones. Leg bones show that later residents walked much further, probably because fields and pastures had moved further away.

Cemeteries around the world also show a sudden rise in the share of young people in the population soon after farming began. Researchers read this as a jump in births, of roughly two more children per woman. Comparing house sizes over time, another team found that differences in wealth generally grew after plants and animals were domesticated, and grew most in Eurasia.

## What's debated

Much of this is inference. The rise in births is read from the ages of skeletons in cemeteries, not counted directly. The pattern is also not the same everywhere: the review of stature found that most, but not all, studies show a decline. Why inequality grew more in Eurasia is argued over. Kohler and colleagues suggest that large domestic animals, such as oxen for ploughing and later horses for warriors, let some households and states grow far richer than others, but this is one explanation among several.

## Why it matters

The change to farming is often told as progress. The bones suggest a more mixed story: more people, but harder lives for many of them, and the start of lasting gaps between rich and poor.
