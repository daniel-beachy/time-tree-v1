## What happened

In 1545, rich silver ore was found in the Cerro Rico, the "rich hill" above Potosí in the Andes, in what is now Bolivia. The historian Kris Lane notes that the find is traditionally credited to an Andean prospector, Diego Huallpa. Under the viceroy Francisco de Toledo, Spanish rulers formalised and greatly expanded an older Andean labour draft, the mita. From 1573 to 1812 it forced Indigenous communities in Peru and Bolivia to send men to work in the mines. Large mills that used mercury to separate silver from crushed ore were built after 1572. In 1592, the year production peaked, Potosí held nearly 100,000 people.

The silver did not stay in Spain. Much of it went to China. In 1571 the Spanish founded Manila in the Philippines, and from then on galleons sailing between Acapulco and Manila carried American silver west and Chinese goods east. Japan also exported large amounts of silver to China until the late 1600s.

## How we know

Historians reconstruct silver output and shipments from surviving colonial and trade records. Dennis Flynn and Arturo Giráldez brought these estimates together to follow silver around the world. They estimate that about 50 tons a year reached China across the Pacific after 1571, while tens of thousands of tons passed through Europe, by sea and overland, on their way to Asia.

## What's debated

Flynn and Giráldez argue that the key cause was in China. When the Ming state moved its money and taxes onto silver, the value of silver there roughly doubled compared with the rest of the world by the early 1500s. Mines in the Americas and Japan expanded to meet that demand. They argue against treating the societies of this period as separate or only weakly connected, and against writing their economic, environmental and population histories apart from one another. How far silver alone drove these connections remains a matter of interpretation.

The mita's effects are still measured today. Melissa Dell compared districts just inside and just outside the old mita boundary in Peru. She found that households inside it consume about a quarter less today, and their children are more often stunted.

## Why it matters

Silver from Potosí and other mines tied the economies of the Americas, Europe and Asia together. It paid for Chinese goods in Europe and the Americas, and Flynn and Giráldez argue that the trade it financed carried American crops into China, helping its population more than double in the 1700s. Much of that silver was mined by Indigenous men under compulsion, through the mita, for more than two centuries.
