## What happened

By the late 600s, during the Tang dynasty, Buddhist texts were being printed in China from wooden blocks. A whole page of text and pictures was carved in reverse into one block, inked, and pressed onto paper. The practice reached Korea and Japan by about 800. The oldest surviving printed book with a date is a copy of the Diamond Sutra, a Buddhist scripture. Its last lines say a man named Wang Jie had it made in 868 for free distribution, on behalf of his parents.

Under the Song dynasty (960 to 1279) the pace of invention quickened. An artisan named Bi Sheng, who died in 1051, made movable type: one small piece of baked clay for each character, which could be set in a frame, printed and reused. Merchants had long traded paper receipts for coins left with deposit shops; in the 1020s the government took over and issued the first state paper money. A military manual of 1044 gives the first true recipe for gunpowder. The scholar Shen Kuo, writing around 1088, gave the first description of a magnetic needle compass, and noted that it does not point exactly north.

## How we know

The Diamond Sutra survived because it was sealed in a cave at Dunhuang, on the Silk Roads in northwest China, some time before about 1000. A monk found the hidden library around 1900, and the scroll is now in the British Library. Bi Sheng's clay type is described in Shen Kuo's book. The gunpowder recipe comes from the 1044 manual itself. No Song paper notes survive; the oldest paper money we can hold is a Ming note of 1375.

## What's debated

The dates of some firsts depend on how they are defined. The compass is a good example: Shen Kuo described the magnetic needle around 1088, but Columbia University's account places the first reports of the compass being used in a new way, with a smaller needle fixed on a stem and sometimes cased for sea travel, in 1119. Movable type is another. In China it was never widely used, because carving whole blocks was cheaper. Korea was casting metal type by the 1230s, and a Buddhist book printed there with metal type in 1377, known as the Jikji, survives in Paris. Whether Johannes Gutenberg, who printed his Bible around 1455, drew on any Asian example is not known.

## Why it matters

Cheap printed books helped literacy spread and fed popular reading and drama in Song cities. Paper money made large trade easier. Gunpowder changed warfare: Song engineers built fire lances, bombs, rockets and early cannons, and the Mongols and other neighbours soon took up the same weapons. Each of these began in China and later reshaped life far beyond it.
