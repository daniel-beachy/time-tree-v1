## What happened

Saint-Domingue, on the Caribbean island of Hispaniola, was a French colony. Its sugar and coffee plantations, worked by enslaved Africans, made it by the 1760s the most profitable colony in the Americas. Enslaved people were the overwhelming majority of its population.

The French Revolution of 1789 split the colony. White colonists divided into royalists and revolutionaries, and free people of mixed race campaigned for civil rights. The enslaved people of the north organised and planned a great rebellion, which began on 22 August 1791.

A decade of many-sided war followed, in which Spanish and British forces also intervened. Toussaint Louverture, one of the revolt's leaders, sided with France after its envoys convinced him that the new French government was committed to ending slavery. By 1801 he controlled the whole colony and issued a constitution. Napoleon sent an army to retake Saint-Domingue and bring slavery back. Louverture was arrested and died in a French prison in 1803. His lieutenant Jean-Jacques Dessalines fought on, the French surrendered, and in 1804 Dessalines declared independence under the name Haiti.

## How we know

This account rests on a museum network's history of the revolution and on the United States government historians' study of how American leaders responded. Those leaders, many of them slave-owners, were torn: at first they sent aid to put the revolt down, and under President John Adams they supported Louverture against his British-backed rivals. Refugees from the burning of the colony's capital in 1793 landed in Norfolk, Baltimore, Philadelphia and New York.

## What's debated

The two sources differ on Louverture's part in 1791: one says he led the uprising, the other calls him one of its leaders. His position was never simple. He insisted that Saint-Domingue remained a French colony even while he ran an independent foreign policy and made himself ruler for life.

The violence is also part of the record. After the French defeat, Dessalines ordered the killing of white colonists who remained. Fear that revolt would spread shaped how the United States treated the new country, although Haiti's leaders were more concerned with survival than with exporting revolution.

## Why it matters

Haiti was the second independent country in the Americas, after the United States, and the only state born from a successful revolt of enslaved people. France's defeat there led it to sell Louisiana to the United States. Recognition came slowly: France recognised Haiti in 1825 and the United States only in 1862.
