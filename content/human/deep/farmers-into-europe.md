## What happened

Farming came to Europe from southwest Asia, and it came mostly by migration. Between about 7000 and 4000 BCE, farming communities spread from the Aegean and western Turkey into the Balkans, up the Danube into central Europe, and along the Mediterranean coasts by sea, setting up farming settlements on the shore. They brought wheat, barley, sheep, goats, cattle and pigs. Hunter-gatherers already living there sometimes took up the new ways, and sometimes kept to the old ones for centuries.

The farmers also brought milking. Cattle were especially important in northwestern Turkey, and fats from milk turn up in cooking pots there and in southeastern Europe by the seventh millennium BCE. Across Europe, milk use was widespread from then on.

## How we know

Ancient DNA is the strongest evidence for migration. Genomes of early farmers from northern Greece and northwestern Turkey closely match those of early farmers across Europe, from the Mediterranean to central Europe. The farmers of Europe were their descendants. Radiocarbon dates from early farming sites get younger with distance from southwest Asia, at roughly a kilometre a year on average.

Milk is detected chemically. Animal fats soak into the walls of unglazed pots, and milk fats can be told apart from meat fats by their chemistry. One study of more than 2,200 vessels found milk by the seventh millennium BCE; a later one used about 7,000 residues from more than 550 sites to map milk use across Europe over 9,000 years.

## What's debated

The ability to digest the milk sugar lactose as an adult is called lactase persistence. It depends on a gene variant that is common in Europe today, and the usual explanation was that milk drinkers had an advantage. But the gene variant was rare among early farmers. It appears around 4700 to 4600 BCE and was not common until about 1000 BCE, thousands of years after milk use became widespread. In 2022 Evershed and colleagues found that the amount of milk people used did not explain the rise. They suggest that drinking milk without the gene was mostly harmless, but became dangerous during famine or epidemics, and that this drove the selection. Not every researcher is convinced.

## Why it matters

The spread of farming into Europe is one of the clearest cases where an idea travelled because people did. And milk, a food that many adults could not digest, became a staple long before their bodies adapted to it.
