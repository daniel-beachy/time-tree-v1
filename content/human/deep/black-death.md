## What happened

In 1346 plague broke out in the Golden Horde, the Mongol state on the steppes north of the Black and Caspian Seas. Over the next seven years it spread around the Black Sea and the Mediterranean and into the lands beyond, reaching Europe, the Middle East and North Africa. The disease is caused by the bacterium *Yersinia pestis*. It survives in populations of wild rodents, from which it can spill over into people. Historians call this outbreak the Black Death, the best-known part of the Second Plague Pandemic.

## How we know

For a long time the evidence was written, and most of it described parts of western Europe. Genetics has added a new kind of proof: ancient DNA from medieval victims has confirmed that *Yersinia pestis* was the cause.

In 2022, Maria Spyrou and colleagues studied two cemeteries near Lake Issyk-Kul in Kyrgyzstan, where tombstones dated 1338 and 1339 name pestilence as the cause of death. They recovered plague DNA from the buried individuals. The strain turned out to be the common ancestor of a sudden burst of new plague lineages, sometimes called the Big Bang, that is linked with the start of the pandemic. Its closest living relatives are found in rodents of the Tian Shan mountains nearby. The team concluded that the Black Death emerged in this region in the early 1300s.

A different kind of evidence comes from pollen. In 2022, Adam Izdebski and colleagues looked at pollen in 261 dated lake and bog cores from 19 European countries. Fewer people meant fewer fields, so a heavy death toll should show up as a fall in crop pollen and a return of trees and grass.

## What's debated

The usual figure is that the Black Death killed up to half the population of the regions it struck. The pollen study found that some regions did see a sharp collapse in farming, but others carried on almost unchanged. That points to large differences in mortality from place to place, so any single figure for Europe, let alone the whole Old World, is uncertain.

The timing of the origin is also argued. The historian Monica Green argues that the burst of plague lineages began around 1200, and that Mongol armies and trade carried plague across Central Eurasia from the thirteenth century, a hundred years before 1346. In her view there were several separate outbreaks, in China, Central Eurasia and the Black Sea and Mediterranean, rather than one wave moving west. Spyrou and colleagues date the burst to the first half of the fourteenth century instead.

## Why it matters

The Black Death has been called the largest pandemic in human history. It also shows how closely the Old World had been tied together by the 1300s: a disease that began among rodents in the mountains of Central Asia reached cities thousands of kilometres away within a few years.
