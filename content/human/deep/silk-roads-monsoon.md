## What happened

From the second century BCE, trade across Asia grew sharply. In 101 BCE a Han Chinese army reached Ferghana in Central Asia, in part to obtain its prized horses. Chinese silk travelled west, while goods such as grapes, wine and alfalfa came east. No single merchant made the whole journey. Goods passed along a chain of traders, through oasis towns such as Kashgar and Samarkand, and across the Parthian empire in Iran toward the Mediterranean.

At the same time, sailors learned to use the monsoon, the seasonal winds of the Indian Ocean that blow one way in summer and the other in winter. Ships sailed from Egypt's Red Sea ports, such as Berenike, to the southwest coast of India and back again within a year. Roman gold and silver paid for Indian pepper, spices, gems and cloth. From India, other ships crossed the Bay of Bengal to Southeast Asia, where goods were carried overland across the narrow Kra isthmus and on toward the South China Sea.

Ideas travelled too. Buddhist monks and merchants carried their faith along these routes into Central Asia and then to China.

## How we know

UNESCO's listing of the Silk Roads describes a network of routes running more than 35,000 kilometres, including a corridor of about 5,000 kilometres linking Chang'an with Central Asia. Han records describe embassies and campaigns. For the sea routes, a Greek merchant's guide called the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea describes Roman coins being exchanged for pepper at the Indian port of Muziris. Tamil poems speak of foreign traders bringing gold for pepper. At Berenike and Myos Hormos on the Red Sea, archaeologists have found writing in Tamil and in Prakrit, a group of Indian languages. Roman goods turn up at Arikamedu and Pattanam in India.

In Southeast Asia, pendants copying Roman coins have been found at Óc Eo in Vietnam and at sites in Thailand. A stone mould shows that some of them were made locally.

## What's debated

Historians argue about how big this trade was. The Roman writer Pliny claimed that 100 million sesterces a year flowed east, but his figure may be a moral complaint about luxury rather than an accurate count. Scholars also debate who ran the trade. Older accounts put Romans at the centre; newer work stresses the role of Indian merchants and rulers.

## Why it matters

These routes linked Han China, the Kushan and Parthian lands, India, Southeast Asia and Rome for the first time in a lasting network. Goods, crops, technologies and religions moved along them for centuries. They are the start of a connected world.
