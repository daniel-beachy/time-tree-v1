## What happened

From the early 1500s to 1866, ships based in Europe and the Americas carried captives from the coast of Africa across the Atlantic to be sold as slaves. SlaveVoyages, the main database of the trade, estimates that about 12.5 million people were forced aboard and about 10.7 million survived to land, almost all of them in the Americas.

The trade began slowly. About 2.2 million people were carried before 1700 and about 2.6 million more between 1700 and 1750. It then grew larger still, and nearly 2 million were carried in each 25-year period from 1751 to 1825.

West-Central Africa was the largest region of departure through most of the trade. Others included Senegambia, the Gold Coast, and the Bights of Benin and Biafra. About 95 percent of those who arrived in the Americas were landed in the Caribbean and South America, and less than 4 percent in North America. About 80 percent were taken to regions that grew sugar. Ships from seven ports, Rio de Janeiro, Bahia, Liverpool, London, Nantes, Bristol and Pernambuco, carried almost three-quarters of all the captives taken from Africa by sea.

## How we know

Slaving voyages left records in archives on both sides of the Atlantic. The SlaveVoyages database gathers records of about 36,000 voyages across the Atlantic, some record of almost 80 percent of the ships that landed captives. From these, David Eltis and colleagues estimate the voyages that left no trace. On the voyages where deaths were recorded, about 12 percent of the people on board died during the crossing, called the Middle Passage. The rate varied from about 9 to nearly 20 percent depending on where the ship had left from.

## What's debated

The SlaveVoyages figures are widely used, but Eltis notes that other estimates of the trade's size exist, and the database is revised as new records come to light. Historians also debate the long-term effects on Africa. Using records of where captives came from, the economist Nathan Nunn found that the African countries from which the most people were taken are poorer today. He tested whether places that were already poorer were simply more exposed to the trade, and concluded that the slave trades held back economic development. Others caution that this kind of link is hard to prove, since places already in turmoil may both have lost more people and grown more slowly.

## Why it matters

About 4.7 million people were taken before 1750, within the period of this chapter, and about 7.8 million after it. Most were taken to regions growing sugar for distant markets, and the plantation economies of the Americas depended on the labour of enslaved Africans and their descendants. The trade bound Africa, Europe and the Americas into one Atlantic system.
