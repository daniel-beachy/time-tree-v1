## What happened

In 550 BCE Cyrus, a ruler from Persia in southwestern Iran, defeated the king of the Medes. Within about a decade he had taken Lydia and its capital, Sardis, in western Anatolia, and in 539 BCE he entered Babylon. His son Cambyses conquered Egypt in 525 BCE. Darius I, who ruled from 522 to 486 BCE, pushed the frontier into northwestern India. The result was the largest empire the world had yet seen, stretching from the Nile to the Indus.

Darius divided the empire into provinces, each run by a governor called a satrap. He built a network of roads with relay stations so that officials and messages could travel fast. Around 518 BCE he began a great terrace at Persepolis, where he and his successors Xerxes I and Artaxerxes I built palaces and halls. Persian rule also changed life for subject peoples. When Cyrus took Babylon, he ended the exile of the Judeans whom the Babylonians had carried off decades before, and many returned to Jerusalem.

The empire fought long wars with Greek cities, losing at Marathon in 490 BCE and at Salamis and Plataea in 480 and 479 BCE. In the 330s BCE Alexander of Macedon conquered it province by province.

## How we know

Much of the written story comes from Greek authors, especially Herodotus, who were often the empire's enemies. He describes the road from Sardis to Susa, saying it had royal stations all along it, 111 stages in all, and took about 90 days to walk. Persian kings left their own inscriptions, and the ruins of Persepolis show their style of rule: stone reliefs of people from many lands bringing gifts.

Coins are another clue. The earliest known coins were made of electrum, a natural mix of gold and silver, and were found at the temple of Artemis at Ephesus, near Sardis. A group of them was buried in a pot around 650 to 625 BCE. They were probably issued by a Lydian king. King Croesus of Lydia, whom Cyrus defeated, later issued separate gold and silver coins.

## What's debated

Because so much of the evidence is Greek, historians debate how far it gives a fair picture. Greek writers often showed the Persians as luxurious and tyrannical. Modern scholars try to balance this with Persian, Babylonian and Egyptian sources. The numbers in Herodotus, such as army sizes, are often doubted, though his description of the road fits other evidence for a royal relay system.

## Why it matters

The Persian Empire showed that one state could hold together many peoples, languages and religions over a vast area. It did so with roads, provinces and local rulers rather than by forcing one culture on everyone. Later rulers of large empires faced the same problem, and many used similar tools.
