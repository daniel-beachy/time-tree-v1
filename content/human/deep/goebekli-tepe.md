## What happened

From about 9600 BCE, on a limestone ridge near the modern city of Şanlıurfa in southeastern Turkey, people built a series of round and rectangular enclosures. Each was ringed by T-shaped stone pillars, cut from the bedrock nearby and set upright, with two larger pillars in the middle. The tallest are over five metres high. Many are carved with animals in relief. Some have arms, hands and belts carved on their sides, which suggests they stood for human-like beings. Building went on, on and off, until about 8200 BCE.

## How we know

The animal bones come from wild species, mostly gazelle, so the builders lived by hunting and gathering. Radiocarbon dates place the oldest enclosures before the first domesticated crops and animals in the region. Large limestone vessels and the pattern of animal bones led the excavators to propose that people met here in large numbers to feast, and that beer brewed from wild grain may have been part of it.

## What's debated

The excavators long called Göbekli Tepe a sanctuary, a place for ritual rather than daily life, and newspapers turned that into "the world's first temple". UNESCO uses more careful language. In 2011 the archaeologist Edward Banning argued that the buildings might have been houses, large and full of symbols, like the decorated houses of later villages such as Çatalhöyük. He pointed out that art does not by itself make a building religious. The excavators replied that the special buildings are the rule at the site, not the exception. How many people lived there all year, and how the work of moving the pillars was organised, are still open questions.

## Why it matters

The usual story was that farming came first, and that villages, surplus food and big monuments followed. Göbekli Tepe turns part of that around. Hunter-gatherers here organised the labour to quarry, carve and raise huge stones, at about the time their neighbours began cultivating wild cereals. The excavators think that gatherings like these, which needed lots of food in one place, may have been one of the pressures that pushed people toward farming. Whether or not that is right, the site shows that complex shared beliefs and big projects did not wait for agriculture.
