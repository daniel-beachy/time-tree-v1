## What happened

When modern humans spread across Europe and Asia, other kinds of human already lived there. Modern humans reached Europe more than 45,000 years ago and shared it with Neanderthals for several thousand years.

They did more than share it. Genomes from people who lived about 45,000 years ago at Ranis in Germany and Zlatý kůň in Czechia carry stretches of Neanderthal DNA. Those stretches trace back to a single period of mixing, dated to about 45,000 to 49,000 years ago, and it is the same mixing whose traces everyone with ancestry outside Africa carries today. The ancestors of all those people, it follows, were still one population at that time.

Further east lived the Denisovans. People met them too: Denisovan DNA survives in several East Asian populations and in Tibetans, who inherited from Denisovans a genetic adaptation to thin air at high altitude.

## How we know

Denisovans are known directly only from fragmentary fossils. Their genomes have been read from a single site, Denisova Cave in Siberia. A jawbone found on the Tibetan Plateau, at least 160,000 years old, was identified as Denisovan from the proteins preserved in it, and showed that they lived far from Siberia and high in the mountains long before modern humans arrived.

The end of the Neanderthals is dated by radiocarbon. One study re-dated 40 sites from Russia to Spain with improved methods and found that the stone-tool tradition linked to Neanderthals ended between about 41,000 and 39,000 years ago.

## What's debated

Dating is hard here because radiocarbon reaches its limit at about 50,000 years. The same study found that Neanderthals disappeared at different times in different regions, and that they overlapped with modern humans in Europe for 2,600 to 5,400 years. The dates alone do not say why they disappeared.

The Ranis and Zlatý kůň people belonged to one small, isolated population, the earliest known branch to split from the lineage of all other people outside Africa. Human remains outside Africa older than 50,000 years seem to come from still other populations.

## Why it matters

The other humans are gone, but not entirely. Part of their DNA lives on in people with ancestry outside Africa, and in Tibet a Denisovan inheritance helps people live at high altitude.
