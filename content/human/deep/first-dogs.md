## What happened

Dogs descend from grey wolves, and they are the only animal known to have joined human life during the ice age. The wolf population they came from appears to be extinct.

One reconstruction places the change in Siberia by about 23,000 years ago. At the coldest point of the last ice age, people and wolves there may have been cut off together from the rest of the world. Dogs then went with the first people across the Bering land bridge into the Americas, and spread with them through the continent from about 15,000 years ago.

By 11,000 years ago dogs had already split into at least five major lineages.

## How we know

Two kinds of evidence are used. Bones are one: the oldest dog that researchers generally accept, from Bonn-Oberkassel in Germany, is about 15,000 years old. Older skulls have been put forward as dogs, but their DNA groups them with ancient wolves, not with any dog lineage.

The other is DNA. One team read the genomes of 27 ancient dogs, up to 10,900 years old, from Europe, the Near East and Siberia. All dogs turned out to share one ancestry, distinct from wolves living today. Another team compared the family trees of ancient dogs with those of ancient people in Siberia, Beringia and North America. The two sets of lineages split and moved in step, which is what points to Siberia and to the date.

## What's debated

The Siberian origin is an inference from how lineages divide, not a find: no securely identified dog remains of that age are known from Siberia, and a few possible ones still await genetic study. Where dogs were first domesticated, and how many times it happened, remain unresolved, as do the circumstances in which wolves and people first came together.

## Why it matters

The dog was the first domesticated species, taken in by hunter-gatherers during the ice age. Its later history follows ours closely. Dogs moved with the first farmers into Europe, and with Arctic peoples across northern North America.
