## What happened

Today more than 500 Bantu languages are spoken by about 310 million people, across most of Africa south of the equator. Languages such as Swahili, Zulu, Shona and Kikongo all belong to this one family. They share so much because their speakers' ancestors came from one small region in west-central Africa, near the border of today's Cameroon and Nigeria.

Starting roughly 5,000 to 4,000 years ago, groups of Bantu speakers began to move. Over the following thousands of years they spread south through the rainforest and along its edges, then east toward the Great Lakes and south toward what is now South Africa. Farming became more and more central to their way of life, and in later centuries many communities also worked iron. They did not move into empty land. Hunter-gatherers and herders already lived in many of these regions, and the newcomers often married into those communities.

## How we know

The evidence comes from two main sources. Linguists compare words across hundreds of living Bantu languages and build family trees showing which languages split from which. By placing each language on a map, they can estimate where and roughly when the splits happened. One study of this kind found that the spread followed open savannah where it could, and that the rainforest slowed it down.

Geneticists compare the DNA of living people. A study of more than 1,700 people found that genetic diversity falls the farther a group lives from western Africa. This is what you would expect if small groups split off again and again as they moved. The same work found that Bantu speakers mixed with local people along the way, and that a region in today's Zambia and the Democratic Republic of the Congo was a crossroads of later movements. DNA from ancient skeletons, though still scarce in Africa, adds further checks.

## What's debated

The biggest question is the route. One view holds that early Bantu speakers waited until the rainforest thinned around 2,500 years ago, opening savannah corridors, and then moved south through them. A 2022 study using a larger language tree argued instead that speakers entered the rainforest about 4,400 years ago, before those corridors opened. The dates in all these studies are wide estimates, and different methods give different answers.

There is also debate about why the spread happened. Farming, a growing population and later iron tools are all possible drivers, and they probably mattered in different ways in different places.

## Why it matters

The Bantu expansion is one of the largest movements of people and languages in human history. It shaped the languages, farming and genes of hundreds of millions of people living today. It also shows that the great changes of these centuries were not limited to empires with written records.
