## What happened

During the ice age, low seas joined Australia, New Guinea and Tasmania into one continent, Sahul, and joined much of island Southeast Asia to the mainland as Sunda. Between them lay Wallacea, a wide belt of islands that was never connected to either side. The gap between the two exposed shelves measured 1,000 to 1,500 kilometres, and getting through it by island hopping took several sea crossings.

No large land mammal had ever made the full crossing and settled. People did. Mitochondrial DNA, which is passed down from mothers, shows that the founders included at least nine or ten separate maternal lineages, and the founding group is estimated at somewhere above 72 to 400 people, probably many more. Numbers like that mean a planned settlement, in boats large enough to carry people and supplies on voyages of several days.

## How we know

At Madjedbebe, a rock shelter in northern Australia, excavators found dense bands of artefacts: flaked stone tools, grinding stones, ground ochre and axes with ground edges. They dated the sand around the lowest band by optical dating, a method that measures how long ago grains of sand last saw sunlight, and concluded that people were there about 65,000 years ago.

Genetic clocks, which estimate dates from differences in the DNA of living people, give a second line of evidence.

## What's debated

The date. A second team argues that an age above 50,000 years for Madjedbebe is unlikely to be valid. The site lies in a sand sheet, and in tropical sand, rain, slow creep and above all termites can move objects downward, so that a tool ends up in sand older than itself. They point out that after decades of searching, Madjedbebe is the only site in Australia still claimed to be older than 50,000 years, and that genetic clocks and other sites put the first settlement of Sahul at about 48,000 to 50,000 years ago.

The excavators considered disturbance and rejected it, citing broken artefacts whose pieces fit back together within the same layer.

If the older date is right, people left Africa earlier than most evidence suggests, or an earlier group reached Australia and left no trace in the DNA of people living today.

## Why it matters

People reached Sahul by sea tens of thousands of years before anyone settled the Americas or the remote Pacific islands. It took boats, planning and enough people to found a lasting population.
