## What happened

Around 3300 BCE, officials in Uruk began marking clay tablets with signs for things and numbers. A typical tablet records amounts of barley, beer, cloth, sheep or workers, and who was responsible for them. This system is called proto-cuneiform. It could not yet record a sentence: it was a tool for keeping accounts in an economy too large for memory. Over the following centuries, scribes began using signs for their sounds as well as their meanings. That let them spell names and, eventually, write down speech. The wedge-shaped script that grew out of it, cuneiform, was used for about 3,000 years and for more than a dozen languages.

## How we know

Excavators at Uruk recovered around five thousand of these early tablets, most of them thrown out as rubbish in ancient times and reused as building fill. The great majority are administrative records. The rest are lists of words, such as names of professions, animals and vessels, which scribes copied to learn the signs. Because the tablets are clay, they survived; records kept on wood, cloth or string elsewhere mostly did not.

## What's debated

Scholars disagree about what came before the tablets. One view holds that small clay tokens, used for counting across the region for thousands of years, developed step by step into written signs. Others see the tokens as only one of several sources. It is also unsettled whether Egyptians invented their hieroglyphs independently or borrowed the idea of writing from Mesopotamia; the earliest Egyptian labels are about as old as the Uruk tablets. Writing in China and in Mesoamerica is agreed to have been invented separately.

## Why it matters

Writing let knowledge outlive the people who held it and travel further than anyone could carry it in memory. Laws, contracts, letters, astronomy, medicine and literature all followed. It also marks a boundary in how we know the past: before writing, we have only objects and bones, and after it, we begin to have names and voices. That it began with accounts says something about what the first cities needed most.
