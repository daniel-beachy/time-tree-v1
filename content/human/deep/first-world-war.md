## What happened

Both sides expected a quick victory. Instead the war in western Europe settled into trenches: a line of ditches and fortifications that at its longest ran some 475 miles (about 760 kilometres), roughly from the North Sea to the Swiss border. Long-range artillery, poison gas, tanks and aircraft killed on a new scale, and commanders were slow to change their tactics. On 1 July 1916, the first day of the battle of the Somme, the British Army alone suffered over 57,000 casualties.

In 1917 Russia had two revolutions and left the war, and the United States joined it. Germany asked for an armistice, an agreement to stop fighting, and the guns on the western front fell silent at 11 a.m. on 11 November 1918.

By then a second disaster was under way. A new influenza spread around the world in three waves within about a year. The first, in the spring of 1918, made many people ill but killed few. The second, from September to November 1918, was highly fatal, and a third followed early in 1919. About a third of the world's people, some 500 million, fell ill.

## How we know

Armies recorded their dead, so military losses are known fairly well: Germany lost about 1.77 million soldiers and Russia about 1.7 million. No official agency kept careful count of civilians.

Influenza was not a disease that doctors had to report in 1918, so its toll is rebuilt from death records, which are missing for much of the world. The virus itself was unknown at the time. In 1995 a team began reading its genetic code from autopsy tissue kept since the autumn of 1918. That work showed the virus was the ancestor of almost every influenza A virus that has circulated in people since.

## What's debated

The number of influenza deaths is still argued over. A calculation in the 1920s put it near 21.5 million. A 1991 study raised that to between 24.7 and 39.3 million, and a 2002 study to about 50 million, adding that the true figure might be twice as high. In 2018 another team, using death records from 13 countries and British India, estimated 17.4 million and judged totals above 25 million unrealistic.

Where the pandemic began is not known: the records are too thin to say. Nor is it settled why this influenza, unlike any before or since, killed so many healthy adults between about 20 and 40 years old.

## Why it matters

The war brought down the German emperor and the Russian tsar, and the harsh peace terms that followed fed the resentment on which the Nazi movement later grew.

By every estimate above, the pandemic killed more people in about a year than died as soldiers in four years of war. Descendants of the 1918 virus still circulate, and it remains a warning for public health.
