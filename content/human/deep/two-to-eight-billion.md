## What happened

Plants need nitrogen in a form their roots can take up, and for all of history farmers had only what soils and a few crops such as peas and beans supplied. That set a ceiling on harvests. In 1908 the German chemist Fritz Haber found a way to turn nitrogen from the air into ammonia, which plants can use. Another German chemist, Carl Bosch, scaled the process up for industry, and factory-made fertiliser went on sale from 1910.

The second change was in the plants themselves. From the 1960s, publicly funded breeders produced high-yielding varieties of wheat, rice and maize and adapted them to the tropics, a campaign known as the Green Revolution. Between 1960 and 2000, yields in developing countries rose by 208 percent for wheat, 109 percent for rice and 157 percent for maize.

Over about fifty years the population of the developing world more than doubled, and its cereal harvest tripled, from only 30 percent more farmland.

## How we know

Nobody can count the people fed by fertiliser directly. Researchers work it out from nitrogen budgets: how much of the nitrogen in crops, and so in the protein people eat, came from factories. One widely used estimate, published in 2008, is that synthetic fertiliser supported 42 percent of all births in the century after its invention, and fed 44 percent of the world's people in 2000. A separate study put the share at about half in 2019.

For the Green Revolution, economists compare what happened with models of a world without the new varieties. One such estimate is that food production in developing countries would have been almost 20 percent lower.

## What's debated

The estimates agree on a range, 40 to 50 percent of humanity, more than on a number. Fertiliser arrived together with better seed, irrigation and machines, and separating their effects is hard.

The Green Revolution's costs are also argued over. Heavy use of water, fertiliser and pesticides degraded soils and polluted water beyond the fields. The new seeds worked best on good, well-watered land, so poorer and drier regions were left behind. In sub-Saharan Africa, varieties brought from Asia and Latin America suited local conditions badly, and suitable ones came only in the 1980s.

## Why it matters

In the 18th century Thomas Malthus argued that population would always outrun food, and in the 1960s famine on a vast scale was widely predicted. It did not happen. The world went from about 1.65 billion people in 1900 to more than 8 billion, and roughly every second person alive today is fed by nitrogen taken from the air.
