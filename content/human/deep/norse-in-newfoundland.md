## What happened

Norse sailors, the seafaring people of Scandinavia often called Vikings, had made homes in Iceland and Greenland. Around the year 1000 some of them sailed further west and reached North America. At L'Anse aux Meadows, on the northern tip of Newfoundland, they built eight buildings with sod walls and roofs over wooden frames, like houses in Iceland and Greenland at the time. They had workshops and a small forge where they smelted iron, the first time iron was smelted in the Americas. Then they left, probably after only a few years, and the buildings decayed.

## How we know

The Norse stories called sagas, first written down in the 1100s and 1200s, tell of voyages from Greenland to a land called Vinland. In one, the Greenlander Leif Eriksson sets up a camp there; in another, a later expedition fights with the local people, whom the Norse called skrælings, and goes home because it is badly outnumbered. For a long time nobody knew whether these stories described real places.

In 1960 the Norwegian explorer Helge Ingstad, guided by a local man, George Decker, found low grassy ridges at L'Anse aux Meadows. He and the archaeologist Anne Stine Ingstad led excavations there for eight years. Iron slag and iron boat rivets showed the site was Norse, because the Indigenous peoples of the area did not work iron then. A spindle whorl and a needle fragment, tools often linked with women's work, suggest that some of the settlers were women.

In 2021, researchers dated three pieces of wood from the Norse layers that had been cut with metal blades. In 993 a burst of cosmic rays raised the amount of radiocarbon in the air for one year, leaving a marker in that year's tree ring. By finding that ring and counting the rings after it to the bark edge, they showed the trees were felled in 1021.

## What's debated

The 1021 date fixes one year, not the first arrival or the length of stay. Reading the two sagas side by side, the dating team estimates that the Norse came and went over somewhere between about three and thirteen years. Because the sagas were written down centuries later, how closely their details match the site is still argued over. Finds at L'Anse aux Meadows show that it served as a base from which regions further south were explored, but where exactly the Norse went is uncertain. People had lived at the site, on and off, for thousands of years before the Norse came.

## Why it matters

L'Anse aux Meadows is the oldest known European site in the Americas, about five hundred years before Columbus. The researchers who fixed the date point out that it also marks the first time humans are known to have circled the globe: the ancestors of Native Americans had crossed from Asia, and now Europeans had reached the same continent from the other direction. The Norse presence itself was brief.
