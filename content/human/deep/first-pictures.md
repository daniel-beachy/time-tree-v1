## What happened

In a limestone cave called Leang Karampuang, on the Indonesian island of Sulawesi, a painted scene shows human-like figures interacting with a pig. It was made at least 51,200 years ago. Its discoverers call it the earliest known surviving example of representational art, meaning a picture of something, and of telling a story in pictures.

It is not alone. A hunting scene in another cave in the same district, Leang Bulu' Sipong 4, is at least about 50,200 years old, and other caves there hold paintings in a similar style.

On the other side of Eurasia, in caves in the Swabian Jura of southwestern Germany, archaeologists found flutes made of bone and of ivory. They date from the time modern humans first settled Europe, more than 35,000 years ago. By then, the finders argue, music was already a well-established tradition.

## How we know

Paint itself is hard to date. In limestone caves, though, water slowly lays down thin crusts of the mineral calcite, sometimes over a painting. The crust contains traces of uranium, which decays at a known rate, so the crust's age can be measured, and the painting beneath must be older.

The Sulawesi team used a newer version of the method, in which a laser samples the crust point by point and maps where the oldest layers lie. Measuring more precisely gave older minimum ages. The hunting scene, first dated to at least 43,900 years, turned out to be at least 4,040 years older than thought.

## What's debated

Every one of these dates is a minimum. The paintings could be much older than their crusts, and the method cannot say by how much.

For music, the evidence is thin because finds are so rare. Some researchers have argued that Neanderthals made music and musical instruments, but the flutes' finders say concrete evidence for that is lacking. Outside the Swabian caves, the earliest secure evidence for music comes from France and Austria and is younger than 30,000 years.

## Why it matters

Pictures of people and animals arranged in a scene, and instruments that took skill to make and to play, are widely taken as signs of fully modern behaviour. The Sulawesi dates show that such picture-making goes back further in our species' history than had been recognised.
