## What happened

People reached the Americas from northeast Asia. Their ancestors lived in and around Beringia, the land that joined Siberia to Alaska when the sea was low. From there they moved south past the North American ice sheets and spread quickly: by about 14,500 years ago there was a camp at Monte Verde in southern Chile, some 15,000 kilometres from Alaska.

## How we know

Three kinds of evidence fit together. Genomes of living and ancient Native Americans show a single founding population that separated from its Asian relatives more than 20,000 years ago. Archaeological sites give minimum dates for when people were present. At Monte Verde, wooden posts, hearths, tools and food remains were preserved under a peat bog. At White Sands in New Mexico, human footprints run through layers of an old lake shore. Seeds in those layers were radiocarbon dated to between 23,000 and 21,000 years ago, and a later study reached similar ages from pollen and from quartz grains, using two other methods.

## What's debated

For most of the twentieth century, the accepted view was "Clovis first": that the earliest Americans were big-game hunters who walked through a gap between the ice sheets about 13,000 years ago. Monte Verde and other sites are older than that, and the gap between the ice sheets probably opened too late, so many researchers now favour a route down the Pacific coast. White Sands pushes the date much further back, to the coldest part of the ice age. Critics questioned the first dates because water plants can take up old carbon and look older than they are. The second study answered that objection for many researchers, but not all, and few other sites of that age have been found.

## Why it matters

The peopling of the Americas completed the spread of our species across the habitable continents. It also began a separation: for more than 15,000 years, people in the Americas and people in Africa, Asia and Europe built farms, cities and states with no knowledge of each other.
