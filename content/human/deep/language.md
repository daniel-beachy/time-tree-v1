## What happened

At some point, humans began to speak in full language: words combined by rules into an unlimited number of sentences. Every human group living today has language of this kind, and none has a simpler or more primitive form. That much is certain. When language began, and whether other kinds of human had it too, is not known. Speech leaves no direct trace, and writing appeared only about 5,000 years ago.

## How we know

All the evidence is indirect. One line comes from genetics. If all living populations share the capacity for language, it probably existed before those populations first split apart. In 2025 Shigeru Miyagawa and colleagues reviewed genome studies that place the earliest split within Homo sapiens at about 135,000 years ago, and argued that the capacity must have been present by then at the latest. A second line compares Neanderthals with us: Dediu and Levinson gathered evidence from their skeletons, genes and archaeology suggesting that they shared something like modern speech. A third comes from archaeology: objects such as beads and engraved patterns suggest people who used symbols, and many researchers think symbols and language are closely tied. Beads from Bizmoune Cave in Morocco show such objects by at least 142,000 years ago.

## What's debated

Almost everything. The usual view in the late twentieth century placed the origin of language between about 100,000 and 50,000 years ago, sometimes as the result of a single sudden genetic change. In 2013 Dan Dediu and Stephen Levinson argued instead that language is much older, going back at least to the common ancestor of Neanderthals and modern humans, about half a million years ago, and that it evolved gradually. Miyagawa's group sets a latest date rather than an earliest one, and suggests that language may have helped bring about the wider spread of symbolic behaviour around 100,000 years ago. These positions rest on different kinds of evidence, and none can be tested directly.

## Why it matters

Language lets people share plans, teach skills, keep track of relatives and allies, and pass on knowledge across generations. Much of what makes human societies possible depends on it. Knowing when it appeared would help explain when and why our ancestors began to behave in ways we recognise. For now, the honest answer is that the capacity was there by around 135,000 years ago, and may be far older.
