## What happened

Before about 2350 BCE, southern Mesopotamia was divided among Sumerian city-states. Each was ruled by a king and centred on the temple of its own god: Ur belonged to Nanna, the moon god, and Uruk to Inanna, goddess of love and war. Later legend says Sargon began as a cupbearer at the court of Kish. He overthrew its king, built a kingdom in the north, then defeated Lugalzagesi of Umma, who had conquered much of Sumer, and united all of Mesopotamia.

Sargon sent Akkadian governors to the Sumerian cities and made Akkadian the official language. Administrative documents say he kept a force of 5,400 men. The Sumerian cities revolted at least twice and were crushed. The empire grew under his grandson Naram-Sin and broke apart after the death of his great-grandson Sharkalisharri.

Sargon made his daughter Enheduanna high priestess of Nanna at Ur. Her first language was probably Akkadian, but she wrote in Sumerian, the language of the south.

## How we know

In 1927 the archaeologist Leonard Woolley found an alabaster disk at Ur. On it a woman, larger than the figures around her, leads a ceremony at an altar. The inscription names her as Enheduanna, priestess of the moon god and daughter of Sargon. About 8,000 clay tablets from the period, royal inscriptions and everyday records of trade and law, show how the empire was run.

Enheduanna's songs survive in later copies. About 100 tablets carry one of them, all written after her death. In two songs to Inanna she speaks in her own voice: "I, En-hedu-ana, will recite a prayer to you."

## What's debated

How Sargon built and held his empire is still unclear. Assyriologists do not know whether it grew from a plan or from a run of opportunities. Enheduanna's poems are some of the hardest Sumerian texts to translate. They use words found nowhere else, and Sumerian has no living relative to compare it with, so readings of them differ.

## Why it matters

Sargon's empire was the first state to bring several peoples, languages and city-states under one ruler. Later kings, Hammurabi of Babylon among them, modelled their reigns on the Akkadian rulers. Enheduanna is the earliest author whose name we know, and the only author in Mesopotamian history of whom a portrait made in her lifetime survives.
