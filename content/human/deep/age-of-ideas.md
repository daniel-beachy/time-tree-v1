## What happened

Between about 600 and 300 BCE, new kinds of teachers appeared in several parts of Asia and the Mediterranean. They asked how a person should live, what makes a good ruler, and how to escape suffering.

In China, Confucius, traditionally said to have lived from 551 to 479 BCE, came from a small state called Lu. He taught that people become good by practising ritual and by cultivating ren, often translated as kindness or humaneness. In the Ganges valley of northern India, the Buddha taught that suffering comes from craving and that it can end by following a middle path between pleasure and harsh self-denial. In the same region, the Jain tradition was taking shape. Judean scribes, after their exile in Babylon, gathered and reworked the writings that became the Hebrew Bible. Greek thinkers debated the nature of the good life, and later schools such as the Stoics taught self-control.

## How we know

Most of what we know comes from texts written down later by followers. The Analects, the main collection of sayings of Confucius, was put together long after his death, and scholars disagree about how much of it records his words. The earliest Buddhist texts were passed on by memory before being written down, so even the Buddha's dates are uncertain. Older estimates put his death around 480 BCE; many scholars now favour a date around 400 BCE.

## What's debated

In the twentieth century the philosopher Karl Jaspers called roughly 800 to 200 BCE the "Axial Age". He saw it as a turning point, when several civilizations independently arrived at similar new ideas. Researchers still argue about whether this is real.

One study argued that these traditions arose in places that had become richer. When people had more than enough to survive, the study suggested, they began to value self-control and long-term goals over immediate rewards. Another study used a large database of past societies to test the idea. It found that features linked to the Axial Age, such as moral codes and limits on rulers' power, appeared in different places at very different times, some centuries or even millennia earlier. That undercuts the idea of a single age.

## Why it matters

Whatever we call this period, the traditions that began in it are still alive. Confucian, Buddhist, Jain, Jewish and Greek ideas shaped the faiths and philosophies of later centuries. Today they influence how billions of people think about right and wrong, family, and the meaning of a good life.
