## What happened

Most people whose ancestry lies outside Africa descend from a migration that began about 60,000 to 50,000 years ago, told in chapter 2. But it was not the first time people of our species left Africa. Fossils from southwest Asia, and perhaps southern Europe, show earlier ventures. A jaw with teeth found in Misliya Cave in Israel has been dated to between 194,000 and 177,000 years ago. A damaged, incomplete skull from Apidima Cave in southern Greece, dated to more than 210,000 years ago, mixes modern and primitive features. Later, between roughly 130,000 and 90,000 years ago, people buried their dead in the caves of Skhul and Qafzeh in Israel.

## How we know

The shape of the Misliya jaw and teeth matches Homo sapiens, and the jaw was found with stone tools made by the Levallois method, a way of preparing a stone core so that flakes of a planned shape can be struck from it. At Skhul, uranium-series and electron spin resonance dates on bones and teeth agree with dates on burnt flint, and place the burials between about 135,000 and 100,000 years ago. Neanderthals lived in the same region around the same time, at nearby Tabun. DNA gives another clue. A Neanderthal thigh bone from Germany carries a type of mitochondrial DNA that suggests Neanderthals received genes from an African population some time between about 270,000 and 100,000 years ago.

## What's debated

Each early find has its doubters. Geologists Warren Sharp and James Paces argued that the uranium-series results for the Misliya jaw had been misread and gave only a minimum age of about 60,000 to 70,000 years. The excavators replied and kept their date. The claim for Apidima rests on one incomplete skull. A broader question is what became of these groups. The later migration accounts for nearly all the ancestry of people outside Africa today, so the early groups seem to have died out or been absorbed. No DNA has been recovered from the fossils themselves to test this.

## Why it matters

These finds show that leaving Africa was not a single event. Groups of our species moved into southwest Asia and perhaps Europe more than once, and met Neanderthals there. Most of these ventures did not last. They help explain why the later, successful migration found a world where the two kinds of human had already met, and why Neanderthal genomes carry traces of early Africans.
