## What happened

Jericho sits beside a large spring on a gentle slope in the Jordan valley. Between about 8300 and 7800 BCE, its early farmers built a stone wall 3.6 metres high around their settlement, and inside it a round stone tower 8.2 metres high, with a staircase of 22 steps inside. The tower weighed around 1,000 tonnes. Later they dug a ditch in front of the wall and added more walls.

About a thousand years later, Çatalhöyük grew up in south-central Turkey. It was lived in from about 7100 to 5950 BCE, and at its height, around 6700 to 6500 BCE, it held between 3,500 and 8,000 people. Mud-brick houses were built wall to wall with no streets between them. People moved across the flat roofs and climbed down into their homes. Many walls inside and out carried paintings and reliefs.

## How we know

At Jericho, Kathleen Kenyon's excavations cut trenches through the mound and exposed the wall and tower. Radiocarbon dates from those layers give the building dates. At Çatalhöyük, modern excavations have studied the houses layer by layer, and the skeletons found there. The bones record diet, disease, injury and how much people walked. The houses show no buildings much grander than the rest, which suggests a society without strong ranks.

## What's debated

Kenyon saw Jericho's wall as a fortification and estimated that 3,000 to 4,000 people lived inside. In 1986 Ofer Bar-Yosef pointed out that the tower stood inside the wall, where it would be of little use to defenders. He proposed that the wall was built in stages to hold back floods and mudflows from a nearby stream, and estimated the population at 400 to 900. At Çatalhöyük, whether to call the settlement a town, a proto-city or a very large village depends on the definition.

## Why it matters

These places show what farming made possible, and what it brought with it. Thousands of people could now live in one place all year. They built together and filled their homes with images. They also lived with crowding: at Çatalhöyük, rubbish pits and animal pens sat beside houses, traces of faeces were found in walls and floors, and healed head wounds became more common when the town was most crowded.
