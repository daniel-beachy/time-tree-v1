## What happened

The Chinese Communist Party was founded in Shanghai in 1921. After years of on-and-off conflict with the Nationalist government, and a full civil war that broke out after the Second World War, its leader Mao Zedong declared the People's Republic on 1 October 1949.

In the late 1950s Mao launched the Great Leap Forward, meant to achieve in a few years the economic advances that had taken other nations decades. Steel was its centrepiece. Tens of millions of peasants were taken from the fields to mine and smelt, and what they made was mostly brittle iron, unfit even for simple tools. Peasants had to give up all private food growing and join communes, which planted less land with grain. Officials reported record harvests that did not exist, and those reports were used to take more grain for the cities. In fact the harvest plummeted. By the spring of 1959 there was famine in a third of China's provinces.

The government took nearly three years to act, and went on exporting food. The famine ended only after 1961, with a return to more workable policies and with imports of grain.

## How we know

The true extent of the famine was not known to the world for two decades. It became clear after the census of 1982, the country's first highly reliable one. It gave the number of people of each single year of age, and the missing people in the famine years could be counted from it.

## What's debated

The first calculations by American demographers put the excess deaths at 16.5 to 23 million. More detailed studies raised that to 23 to 30 million. The dataset used by Our World in Data gives 36 million for 1958 to 1962, and the historian Frank Dikötter has argued for 45 million. Official figures for those years understate the deaths, so the true toll will never be known. About as many births again were lost or postponed.

Drought played a part, but the scholar cited here calls the famine largely man-made, and holds Mao most responsible. He adds that its causes have never been openly discussed in China itself.

## Why it matters

It was the largest famine in human history. What followed changed China again. After President Nixon's visit in 1972, the first business deal signed was for 13 of the world's largest fertiliser plants. In 1979, less than three years after Mao's death, reformers dissolved the communes and freed farm prices. By 1984 food rationing in the cities had ended, and the average food supply per person was within 5 percent of Japan's.
