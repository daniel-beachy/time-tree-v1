## What happened

Around 100 BCE, a city began to grow in the northeast of the Valley of Mexico. Its builders laid it out on a grand plan along a broad avenue. Between about 100 and 250 CE they raised enormous monuments, including the Pyramid of the Sun, which measures about 225 by 222 metres at its base and rises about 75 metres. Later, between about 250 and 550 CE, most people lived in large walled apartment compounds. By around 400 CE, Teotihuacan covered about 20 square kilometres and was the largest city in the Americas.

Around 550 CE, fire destroyed much of the city centre. People went on living there, but in much smaller numbers.

To the southeast, in the lowlands of today's Guatemala, Belize and southern Mexico, Maya cities such as Tikal grew powerful. Tikal had been settled since about 600 BCE. Its rulers raised carved stone monuments, called stelae, recording their births, reigns and wars.

## How we know

Archaeologists have mapped Teotihuacan in detail and excavated many of its compounds. Objects in Teotihuacan style turn up at distant sites such as Tikal, Copán and Monte Albán. Maya inscriptions at Tikal describe ties with Teotihuacan.

The Maya and their neighbours used a Long Count calendar, which counts days from a fixed starting point. The earliest known Long Count date, carved on a monument at Chiapa de Corzo in southern Mexico, corresponds to 36 BCE. The system was inherited from earlier peoples of the Gulf Coast region. Early examples, up to about 162 CE, do not show a zero. Later, a sign for zero appeared, first as a placeholder and later as a number in its own right. This was worked out separately from the zero used in India.

## What's debated

Nobody knows how many people lived in Teotihuacan. UNESCO gives at least 25,000. The Metropolitan Museum of Art cites estimates of 125,000 to 200,000 around 400 CE. The difference depends on how many people are thought to have lived in each compound.

It is also unclear who burned the city centre around 550 CE, or why. Scholars also debate how much power Teotihuacan held over Maya cities: whether it ruled them, traded with them, or mainly served as a powerful example.

## Why it matters

Teotihuacan and the Maya cities show that the Americas developed large cities, calendars and writing on their own, without contact with Asia, Africa or Europe. Their zero is a reminder that a key idea in mathematics was invented more than once.
