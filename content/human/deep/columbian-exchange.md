## What happened

For thousands of years after the ice age, the peoples of the Americas and those of Africa, Asia and Europe grew different crops and lived with different diseases. Ocean voyages after 1492 ended that separation within a few generations. Historians call the result the Columbian Exchange: the transfer of crops, diseases, ideas, technologies and people between the two halves of the world.

From the Americas came four staple crops: maize, potatoes, sweet potatoes and cassava (also called manioc). They gave more calories, or grew in places where older staples did poorly. Tomatoes, chili peppers, cacao, peanuts and pineapples added little in calories but changed cooking across the world, from paprika in Hungary to curries in India and chili in Korea and Thailand. Tobacco went the same way. In the other direction went wheat, rice, sugar cane, coffee, soybeans, oranges and bananas. Diseases travelled too, with terrible effect in the Americas.

## How we know

Much of the evidence is the world's diet today. Nathan Nunn and Nancy Qian point out that the top consumers of potatoes and of cassava are now all countries outside the Americas, and that maize supplies a large share of daily calories in many of them. Written records track when crops arrived: Flynn and Giráldez note that American crops reached Chinese farms through the Manila galleons, the Spanish ships that crossed the Pacific each year from 1565.

To measure effects, economists compare places that could grow a new crop with places that could not, before and after it arrived. Using this method, Nunn and Qian estimate that the potato explains about 12 percent of the rise in population, and nearly half the rise in the share of people living in cities, in the Old World countries suited to it.

## What's debated

The size of the crop effects depends on the method and the period chosen, so figures like these are estimates, not settled facts. The origin of venereal syphilis, which spread across Europe in the 1490s, was argued over for centuries. Nunn and Qian review genetic work by Harper and colleagues that places the bacterium's closest relatives in South America, which supports an American origin, though accounts of similar symptoms and skeletons with similar-looking scars from before 1492 kept the question open for a long time.

## Why it matters

Few people today eat a meal that the Columbian Exchange did not shape. New crops helped feed growing populations in China, Africa and Europe. At the same time, the exchange carried the diseases that emptied much of the Americas, and the plantation crops whose demand for labour drove the transatlantic slave trade. The same voyages that enriched diets on one side of the world brought catastrophe to the other.
