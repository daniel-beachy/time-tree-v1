## What happened

Around 3300 BCE a new archaeological culture, the Yamnaya, appeared on the steppes north of the Black and Caspian Seas. By 3000 BCE it stretched from Hungary in the west to Kazakhstan in the east. Its people were herders. Ancient DNA shows that about four-fifths of their ancestry came from people who had lived between the Caucasus and the lower Volga, and the rest from hunter-gatherers of eastern Europe.

About 4,500 years ago the effects reached the heart of Europe. People of the Corded Ware culture in what is now Germany traced about three-quarters of their ancestry to the Yamnaya. This was a large migration, not only the spread of new goods or ideas, and steppe ancestry is still found in present-day Europeans.

## How we know

The evidence comes from ancient genomes. In 2015 one team read DNA from 69 people who lived in Europe between 8,000 and 3,000 years ago and traced how its populations mixed and replaced one another. A 2025 study added 435 people from the steppe and the lands around it to find where the Yamnaya's own ancestors came from.

Languages leave no bones, so linking a language to a migration is an inference. Genetics shows where people moved and when. Linguists compare core vocabulary across the Indo-European languages to estimate when and where their common ancestor was spoken.

## What's debated

Where the Indo-European languages began is one of the longest-running arguments about the past. One view holds that they spread with horse-using herders from the steppe about 6,000 years ago; another, that they spread with the first farmers out of Anatolia about 9,000 years ago. A 2023 study of core vocabulary dated the family's root to about 8,100 years ago, too early for the steppe view. It proposed a homeland south of the Caucasus, with a later branch that moved north onto the steppe and then across Europe.

The 2025 DNA study proposes instead that the shared ancestor of Hittite and the other Indo-European languages was spoken by the people between the Caucasus and the lower Volga, some time between 4400 and 4000 BCE. Both views accept that the steppe migrations carried at least some Indo-European languages into Europe.

## Why it matters

Within a few centuries the steppe migrations reshaped the ancestry of a continent. They are also a clear case of ancient DNA changing an old argument: a question once argued only from words and pottery can now be tested against the genomes of the people themselves.
