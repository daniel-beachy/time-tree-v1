## What happened

The peoples of the Americas had been separated from Africa, Asia and Europe since the end of the ice age. They had never been exposed to smallpox, measles, whooping cough, chicken pox, typhus or malaria. When these diseases arrived after 1492, they spread through populations with no immunity. Outbreaks came again and again over the following century and more, alongside conquest and war. During the siege of Tenochtitlan in 1521, defenders died of disease and hunger as well as in battle.

The Taíno of Hispaniola, among the first peoples to meet Columbus's crews, had almost disappeared within fifty years. Central Mexico, with just under 15 million people in 1519, had about 1.5 million a century later. The historian Noble David Cook estimated that the least affected regions lost 80 percent of their people, the worst affected lost everyone, and a typical society lost 90 percent.

## How we know

Written records begin only after contact. Disease often travelled ahead of the Europeans who wrote things down, so the earliest losses went unrecorded and first-hand counts may be too low.

Ancient DNA now helps identify the killers. In 2018, Åshild Vågene and colleagues studied a cemetery at Teposcolula-Yucundaa in Oaxaca, linked to an epidemic of 1545 to 1550 that people in Mexico called *cocoliztli*. They recovered genomes of *Salmonella enterica* Paratyphi C, a cause of enteric fever, from ten of the dead, making it a strong candidate for that outbreak.

The scale also shows up in the natural world. In 2019, Alexander Koch and colleagues combined 119 published population estimates with evidence of land use. They estimate about 60.5 million people lived in the Americas in 1492 and that about 90 percent died within a century, roughly 56 million people. As fields were abandoned, forest and grassland grew back on about 56 million hectares and took up carbon from the air.

## What's debated

How many people lived in the Americas in 1492 is one of the longest arguments in the field. Estimates have ranged from about 8 million (Kroeber, 1939) to over 110 million (Dobyns, 1966), and Nunn and Qian note that decades of research have not narrowed the range. Koch and colleagues give a likely range of about 45 to 78 million. Because early observers may have seen populations already thinned by disease, both high and low estimates have kept their defenders.

## Why it matters

Nunn and Qian describe the devastation as far greater than that of the Black Death in Europe. The loss of so many workers, together with demand for plantation crops, helped drive the transatlantic slave trade. Koch and colleagues argue that the regrowth of forests on abandoned land helps explain half or more of a drop in carbon dioxide in the late 1500s and early 1600s, a sign that human history and the Earth's climate were already tied together.
