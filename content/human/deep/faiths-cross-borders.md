## What happened

About 262 BCE Ashoka, ruler of the Maurya empire in India, conquered the neighbouring country of Kalinga. His own inscription counts the cost: 150,000 people deported, 100,000 killed, and many more dead from other causes. Afterwards, it says, he turned to the Dhamma, the Buddha's teaching, and held that the best conquest is conquest by it. He had edicts carved on rocks and pillars across his lands, and he records sending envoys south as far as Tamraparni, an old name for Sri Lanka, and west to the Greek kings.

Buddhism then travelled with trade. Caravan merchants and wealthy bankers were among the Buddha's earliest donors, and patrons hoped in return for refuge and protection on their journeys. Between the first and fifth centuries CE, trade between India, Central Asia and China and the spread of the faith fed each other. Merchants and pilgrims had brought Buddhist ideas to China by the second century CE, among them monks and monasteries, which were new there.

Christianity began as a small movement in first-century Palestine. Recorded congregations numbered 54 by the year 100, 111 by 200 and 594 by 304. In 313 the emperors Constantine and Licinius ruled that anyone who wished to follow the Christian religion could do so freely. Under Theodosius I, who reigned from 379 to 395, it became the Roman Empire's official religion and others were banned.

## How we know

Ashoka was long known only from legends. In 1837 James Prinsep deciphered an ancient inscription on a stone pillar in Delhi, and it and others like it turned out to be the king's own edicts.

For Buddhism, inscriptions at shrines and monasteries in India record gifts from merchants. For Christianity, one study took the congregations mapped in an atlas of the early church and set them on a model of the Roman Empire's roads, rivers and sea lanes linking 648 cities. In the first two centuries the new faith appeared where the travel network and city sizes predict.

## What's debated

Parts of the edicts are far from clear in meaning, so translations differ. Ashoka seems to have called himself a Buddhist before the Kalinga war, and one translator suspects his early commitment was lukewarm and perhaps political.

Nobody counted early Christians. One estimate is 2.5 to 3.5 million by 300 CE, about 5 percent of the empire's 60 million people, and its authors call it conservative and uncertain. Whether Christianity was mainly a religion of cities has also been challenged.

## Why it matters

Both faiths outgrew the places and peoples they began among, carried by roads, ships and the backing of rulers. Buddhism spread through Central and Southeast Asia, China, Korea and Japan. The next chapter adds a third such faith, Islam.
