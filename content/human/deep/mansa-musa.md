## What happened

From the 700s to the 1500s, caravans of hundreds of camels crossed the Sahara. They carried gold, salt, textiles and jewellery between West Africa and the Mediterranean world. Sijilmasa, in what is now Morocco, stood at the northern edge of the desert; Tadmekka and Timbuktu, in today's Mali, stood at the southern edge, linked to the Niger River. West African gold was prized for its purity and was used to mint coins.

In the early 1300s the empire of Mali spread over lands that today lie in Mauritania, Senegal, Mali, Niger and beyond. In 1324 its ruler, Mansa Musa, set out on the hajj, the pilgrimage to Mecca that Islam asks of every Muslim who is able to make it. On the way he stayed in Cairo, then ruled by the Mamluk sultans. When he returned, he had the Djinguereber mosque built in Timbuktu with the help of scholars and an architect from Andalusia, in Muslim Spain, who came back with him.

## How we know

Much of what we know about the visit comes from al-Umari, a 14th-century Arab historian who wrote about it at least a decade later. He reports that Musa said he had come for the pilgrimage and nothing else, that he sent loads of unworked gold to the sultan's treasury, and that he gave gold to every emir and officeholder at court. He adds that before the visit a gold coin, the mithqal, was worth at least 25 silver dirhams in Egypt, and that for about twelve years afterwards it was worth no more than 22.

Archaeology fills in the trade itself. Excavations at Sijilmasa, Gao and Tadmekka have found glazed pottery, glass, beads and silk that crossed the desert. Finds in Mali suggest Arabic was spreading south of the Sahara by the 900s. The manuscripts of Timbuktu show scholars there sharing in the learning of the wider Islamic world.

## What's debated

The most famous details are the hardest to check. Modern accounts often repeat that Musa travelled with 8,000 courtiers, 12,000 servants and a hundred camel loads of gold, and he is often called the richest person who ever lived. Numbers like these are repeated widely but are hard to verify, and wealth across seven centuries cannot be compared in any exact way. Al-Umari's report of the gold price is more specific, but it describes one market, in Cairo, over a decade.

## Why it matters

West African gold was central to the medieval economy of the Mediterranean world. The trade carried more than gold: Islam, Arabic writing and books crossed the Sahara with the caravans, and Timbuktu drew scholars from across the Islamic world. Medieval West Africa was one of the hubs of the connected Old World.
