## What happened

Between about 9500 and 3000 BCE, people in many parts of the world began to cultivate plants and keep animals, each group working with what grew around them. Researchers count at least eleven regions where farming started independently, and several more have been suggested.

In southwest Asia it was wheat, barley, lentils and peas, with goats, sheep and cattle. In northern China, broomcorn millet was being stored at Cishan by about 8300 to 6700 BCE, and rice was slowly domesticated along the Yangtze. In the highlands of New Guinea, people at Kuk Swamp were clearing forest and tending plants by about 8000 BCE and growing bananas in mounds by about 5000 BCE. In Mexico, squash was domesticated by 8,000 years ago and maize by about 6700 BCE. In the Bolivian Amazon, manioc and squash were grown more than 10,000 years ago. In eastern North America, squash, sunflower and two seed plants called marshelder and chenopod were domesticated from about 5,000 years ago. In eastern Sudan, pottery from about 3500 to 3000 BCE carries the imprints of domesticated sorghum.

## How we know

Domestication leaves marks on plants and animals. Wild cereals scatter their seeds when ripe; domesticated ones keep them on the stalk until harvest, and the break left on the grain shows which kind it was. Squash seeds and stalks grow larger. Starch grains and tiny silica bodies called phytoliths survive on tools and in soil long after the plant has rotted. Impressions of chaff in pottery preserve the shape of grain. Radiocarbon dating of the remains themselves gives the timing, and the genes of modern crops point to where their wild ancestors grew.

## What's debated

Two questions run through the field. One is how fast domestication happened. Some once argued it could take as little as a century or two; the archaeological evidence for wheat, barley and rice now points to two to four thousand years of slow change. The other is how to tell an independent centre from a place that received crops or ideas from elsewhere. Genes can flow between crops and their wild relatives, which blurs the trail. Eastern North America was long thought to have borrowed its crops from Mexico before the evidence showed they were local.

## Why it matters

Farming began again and again, on different continents with different plants, within a few thousand years after the ice age. It was not one lucky discovery that spread from a single cradle. The crops of these centres still feed the world: wheat, barley, rice, maize, millet, sorghum, squash, bananas and manioc are among them. And because each region started with its own plants and animals, the farming societies that grew from them took different paths, a difference that shaped the rest of history.
