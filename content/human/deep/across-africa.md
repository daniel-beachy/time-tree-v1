## What happened

For much of the twentieth century, the search for the first members of our species focused on East Africa. The oldest fossils that looked fully modern came from there, and the story was told as if Homo sapiens had appeared in one place at one time. That picture has changed. The oldest fossils now assigned to our species come from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, about 315,000 years ago. A skull from Florisbad in South Africa is about 260,000 years old. In Ethiopia, the Omo I skull, once dated to about 197,000 years ago, is now known to be at least 233,000 years old. Early fossils with some modern features are spread from one end of Africa to the other.

## How we know

The dates come from several methods. At Jebel Irhoud, flint tools burnt in ancient fires were dated by thermoluminescence, which measures the energy stored in crystals since they were last heated. At Omo, the fossil lies beneath a layer of volcanic ash. In 2022 a team matched that ash to a large eruption of the Shala volcano in the Ethiopian Rift and dated the eruption, which gave a minimum age for the skull. The fossils do not all look alike. The Jebel Irhoud people had faces like ours but long, low braincases. A rounder braincase seems to have come later. Genetic studies of living Africans also show divisions between populations that go back a very long way.

## What's debated

In 2018 Eleanor Scerri and colleagues argued that our species did not evolve in a single population or region. They pointed to the spread of the fossils, to regional styles of stone tools that lasted a long time, and to climate records showing that habitable areas kept splitting and joining. In this view, groups lived apart for long periods and then met and mixed again, and modern humans are the result of that whole network. A 2021 review of the genetic evidence reached a similar conclusion: no single point in time can yet be identified when our ancestors were confined to one small birthplace. Other researchers still give more weight to a single region. There are very few fossils, so every view rests on thin evidence.

## Why it matters

The idea of a single cradle is simple and easy to draw on a map, but the evidence fits a messier story. If our species formed across a whole continent, then the deep roots of every person alive today lie in many parts of Africa at once. It also means that the deep splits between populations in Africa today belong to the same long history.
